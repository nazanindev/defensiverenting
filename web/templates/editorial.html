Editorial standards

These are the rules every guide on RenterLaw follows. They say what backs a statement, what we add to the law in our own words, and how every sentence is written. Most of them run as code: a draft that breaks one cannot be saved.

Every statement links to its source

A guide is a list of statements. Under each statement is a source line. It names the exact document the statement comes from, with the section or page, and links to it. The AI that drafts our guides will not save a citation unless the quoted words appear in that document word for word. For a law, the stored quote is the whole subsection, not one sentence from it.

After each citation, a few plain words say what kind of document it is:

  • Law. A statute or ordinance, the law itself. This is the strongest source.
  • An agency rule. A regulation written by a government agency under a law.
  • A government guide. A court, agency, or city page explaining a rule or a program. A few states only put their official laws online through a paid legal publisher. In those states we quote a copy of the law that a state office published, such as the attorney general or the courts. We name the office and how current its copy is, and we check that no newer law changed the words.
  • A court decision. A ruling by a court.
  • A nonprofit group. A legal aid group or nonprofit describing its own services. We use these for local help listings, not for what the law says.
  • Our editorial rules. This page. What it means is explained next.

We never cite law firm websites, legal content sites, or archived copies of a page. We read some of them to find the right law. Then we cite the law.

When a source line says "Our editorial rules"

A statement can cite this page in two ways.

On its own. A small number of statements are practical advice, not law. For example: put every request to your landlord in writing and keep a copy. Take dated photos before you move in. Advice like this is what legal aid lawyers tell renters everywhere. It cannot be traced to one section of law, so its only source is this page. We publish it only when it applies in every place and matches what legal aid groups recommend. If a statement can be backed by a law instead, we do that and drop the editorial source.

Next to a law. Many statements cite a law and this page together. The law backs the rule. This page backs the words we added so the rule is safe to act on. Those words are only the ones listed below. Our rules never back a legal claim.

  • A worked dollar example. The law says "2 times the amount". We add "if the landlord kept $800, that is $1,600". The numbers are ours. The rule is the law's.
  • The win-and-pays line. Some statements say a court can make your landlord pay you money. Each of those also carries one more line. "You get this money only if you win your case and your landlord pays." Nobody should read that money as money that simply arrives.
  • A risk warning. When a statement tells you a step the law allows that a court can later judge, the same statement says the risk. If you can hold back rent or pay less while you stay: "If a court later disagrees, you can owe the rent and face eviction. Get legal help first." If you can end the lease or move out: "If a court later disagrees, you can still owe the rent. Get legal help first." The warning sits on the statement itself, because statements also appear alone on other pages.
  • The owing-nothing reminder. Some statements say you owe no rent, money, or fee. Each of those also says a landlord can still sue, and a court can decide against you.
  • The inspection caution. Some statements tell you to ask for a code inspection. Each of those also says what can follow: for very bad conditions, an inspector can declare the home unfit and order everyone out.
  • A caution from legal aid. A sentence that starts "One caution from legal aid:" on a breaking-the-lease page.
  • A tip. A short practical line under a statement, marked Tip: how to send a notice, what counts as a written record, what to keep.

Two more rules shape this advice. We never tell a renter to call the police. Where calling them is one option, the statement says it is your choice and gives another route beside it. A sentence about being in danger is left as it is. And a text or an email counts as writing: when a statement says to put something in writing, it says so.

How every sentence is written

Guides are written for a renter who is stressed, short on time, and reading on a phone, often in a second language. These rules run when a draft is saved. A statement that breaks one is rejected and rewritten.

  • Short sentences. No sentence is longer than 25 words. One fact per sentence.
  • Short statements. A statement is one claim you can act on, at most 120 words. A rule, who it does not cover, and what you can do about it are three statements. On a few topics, such as breaking a lease, the page says the risk once at the top. There each statement is at most 75 words, and a page has at most 12 statements.
  • Everyday words. A statement must read at about a grade 7 level. Any word outside everyday English is rejected unless it is an official term explained right after it in plain words. We say "take out of your deposit", not "deduct", and "hold back rent", not "withhold".
  • Official terms keep their name, with a gloss. Some words you will meet on court papers, a program application, or your lease. We name them, then explain them in the same statement. For example: "a notice to quit (a letter saying you must move out)", "utilities (water, electric, gas)", "materially (seriously)". A gloss uses only everyday words. It never reuses the word it explains.
  • No legal grammar. Formal legal words that feel normal to lawyers are replaced with what happens. We write "the court will not enforce it", "give up", "must", "end", "the home", and "limit the loss". Money a court awards is "money the landlord must pay you". Harm to the home is "damage".
  • Who is covered. We never say a rule has carve-outs. We name who is left out.
  • Numbers as digits. "14 days", not "fourteen days".
  • We do the math. Any percentage or multiplied amount carries a worked dollar example: "5% of $1,000 rent is $50".
  • One deadline at a time. Every deadline says who gets the days, from when, and what happens next: "counted from the day you hand the keys back". Steps in a sequence are written in order. Separate cases are separate statements.
  • The common case first. Within each stage of a guide, the usual case comes first. Narrow cases and special protections come last. Two statements that say the same rule are merged.
  • Each statement stands alone. It names its own subject and never says "this" or "also" pointing at another statement, because statements are shown alone on other pages.
  • Plain beats short. An edit that makes a statement harder to read is rejected, even when it makes it shorter. A long statement is split or loses a fact. It is never made denser.
  • No dashes. We do not join sentences with a long dash. We use a period, a comma, or a colon.
  • Easy to translate. No idioms or figures of speech. Active voice. The reader is "you". The actor is named: your landlord, the court, the city.

How a guide is checked

AI researches the law and writes the first draft. We use Claude, made by Anthropic. It will not save a citation unless the quoted words appear in the source word for word.

A second reader, also AI, reads each statement against the quotes stored on that statement, never against the rest of the page. A statement passes only when every sentence is said by a stored quote, or is one of the additions listed above. A dropped condition, an added fact, or a gloss that adds meaning is a fail. A failed statement is fixed and read again, or held for a person to decide.

Every change to a statement is logged, with who made it and the rule that allowed it.

An editor reads every draft. A doubt the editor or the AI cannot settle is a flag on the page, and a flagged page cannot be published. A person publishes every page. Nothing goes live without that step. The foot of each guide says who published it and when.

Sources are re-checked over time

Laws change. We fetch each source again every week and compare it to what we quoted. When every source behind a guide still matches, the guide shows a "Sources checked" date. When the words we quoted are gone, the date disappears and the guide is flagged for review.

Not legal advice

No statement on this site is legal advice for your situation, whether it is backed by a law or by our editorial rules. If your housing is at risk, contact a lawyer or a legal aid office near you.

Laws change. Each guide shows when a person published it and when we last checked its sources. Read the source before you act.

Your facts matter. A rule can depend on your lease, your building, or your city. A guide cannot cover every case.

We work to get every statement right, but we cannot promise a guide has no mistakes.

Tell us when something is wrong

Every guide has a link to report a problem. A person reads every report and checks the source before changing anything.
