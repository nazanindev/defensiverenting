Editorial standards

These are the rules every guide on RenterLaw follows. They are how you can tell what backs a statement, and how much to trust it.

Every statement links to its source

A guide is a list of statements. Under each statement is a source line. It names the exact document the statement comes from, with the section or page, and links to it. The AI that drafts our guides will not save a citation unless the quoted words appear in that document word for word.

After each citation, a few plain words say what kind of document it is:

  • Law. A statute or ordinance, the law itself. This is the strongest source.
  • An agency rule. A regulation written by a government agency under a law.
  • A government guide. A court, agency, or city page explaining a rule or a program. A few states only put their official laws online through a paid legal publisher. In those states we quote a copy of the law that a state office published, such as the attorney general or the courts. We name the office and how current its copy is, and we check that no newer law changed the words.
  • A court decision. A ruling by a court.
  • A nonprofit group. A legal aid group or nonprofit describing its own services. We use these for local help listings, not for what the law says.
  • Our editorial rules. Editorial guidance. See below.

Editorial guidance

A small number of statements are practical advice, not law. For example: put every request to your landlord in writing and keep a copy. Take dated photos before you move in.

Advice like this is what legal aid lawyers tell renters everywhere. It cannot be traced to one section of law, so its source line says "Our editorial rules" and links to this page. We only publish it when it applies in every place and matches what legal aid groups recommend. If a statement can be backed by a statute instead, we do that and drop the label.

How a guide is checked

AI researches the law and writes the first draft. We use Claude, made by Anthropic. It will not save a citation unless the quoted words appear in the source word for word.

A second reader, also AI, reads each statement against its source. It records the exact passage that backs the statement. If it cannot find one, the statement is fixed and read again, or held for a person to decide.

Every change to a statement is logged, with who made it and the rule that allowed it.

A person publishes every page. Nothing goes live without that step. The line at the top of each guide says who published it and when.

Sources are re-checked over time

Laws change. We fetch each source again every week and compare it to what we quoted. When every source behind a statement still matches, the statement shows a "Sources checked" date. When the words we quoted are gone, the statement loses that date and the guide is flagged for review.

Plain language

Guides are written for a renter who is stressed, short on time, and reading on a phone. Short sentences. Common words. When a legal term appears, we explain it in the same sentence. Deadlines say what starts the clock. Percentages come with a worked dollar example.

Not legal advice

No statement on this site is legal advice for your situation, whether it is backed by a statute or marked as editorial guidance. If your housing is at risk, contact a lawyer or a legal aid office near you.

Laws change. Each guide shows when a person published it. Each statement shows when we last checked its sources. Read the source before you act.

Your facts matter. A rule can depend on your lease, your building, or your city. A guide cannot cover every case.

We work to get every statement right, but we cannot promise a guide has no mistakes.

Tell us when something is wrong

Every guide has a link to report a problem. A person reads every report and checks the source before changing anything.
